# NAD⁺ precursors in humans — the disconfirming tier

Captured: 2026-09-02 (abstracts via NCBI eutils; full texts not fetched — see LIMITS)

Relevance: the Track 2 report's **highest-ranked candidate overall is nicotinamide
riboside**, a dietary supplement. The repository already records one awkward negative
(Shoji 2025, Werner syndrome RCT: sarcopenia indicators unchanged). This file exists to
establish that **the Werner result is not an isolated miss but the consistent pattern
across independent human RCTs**: NAD⁺ precursors reliably engage their target and
reliably fail to move muscle functional endpoints. See
`literature/_contradiction_matrix.md`, conflict **C3**.

Assembled by deliberately searching for null and negative results, per the review's
requirement that an evidence base finding only supporting studies has not searched hard
enough.

---

## The trials

**Stocks B et al. 2022, *JCI Insight* 7(19):e156925 — PMID 35998039.** *Directly the most
relevant negative.* 32 elderly individuals (55–80), randomised, placebo-controlled, 1000 mg
NR + 200 mg pterostilbene daily; experimental muscle injury by electrically induced
eccentric work; biopsies to 30 days. Verbatim: "MuSC content, proliferation, and cell size
revealed a large demand for recruitment after injury, but **this was not affected by
NRPT**. Furthermore, histological analyses of muscle fiber area, central nuclei, and
embryonic myosin heavy chain showed **no NRPT supplementation effect**." Conclusion:
"safe but does not improve recruitment" of muscle stem cells.

**Elhassan YS et al. 2019, *Cell Reports* 28(7):1717 — PMID 31412242.** 12 aged men,
1 g NR/day, 21 days, placebo-controlled crossover. Target engagement succeeded — "NR
elevated the muscle NAD+ metabolome" — but "Muscle RNA sequencing revealed NR-mediated
downregulation of energy metabolism and mitochondria pathways, **without altering
mitochondrial bioenergetics**." Positive finding: "NR also depressed levels of circulating
inflammatory cytokines."

**Dollerup OL et al. 2018, *Am J Clin Nutr* 108(2):343 — PMID 29992272.** 40 obese
insulin-resistant men, 1000 mg NR twice daily, 12 weeks, parallel-group. "Insulin
sensitivity, endogenous glucose production, and glucose disposal and oxidation **were not
improved** by NR supplementation."

**Remie CME et al. 2020, *Am J Clin Nutr* 112(2):413 — PMID 32320006.** 13 overweight/obese
adults, 1000 mg/d NR, 6 weeks, crossover with hyperinsulinaemic-euglycaemic clamps and
muscle biopsies. Markers of NAD⁺ synthesis rose in skeletal muscle; NR increased fat-free
mass (+1.34% ± 0.50%, P = 0.02), sleeping metabolic rate and muscle acetylcarnitine — but
the trial's primary metabolic targets (insulin sensitivity, mitochondrial function) were
not improved.

**The counterweight — McDermott MM et al. 2024, *Nat Commun* 15:5046 — PMID 38871717
(NICE trial).** 90 people with peripheral artery disease, 6 months NR ± resveratrol. NR
"significantly improved 6-min walk (+7.0 vs. −10.6 meters, between group difference:
+17.6 (90% CI: +1.8, +∞)"; per-protocol +31.0 m. The authors' own closing sentence: "A
larger clinical trial to confirm these findings is needed."

## Adjudication

Weighing these against each other and against Shoji 2025 (`literature/21_...`):

1. **Target engagement is not in doubt.** Four independent trials measuring the muscle or
   blood NAD⁺ metabolome found it rises on oral NR. Any claim that NR "does not reach
   muscle" is refuted.
2. **Muscle *functional* benefit is null in the trials designed to detect it.** Stocks 2022
   (regeneration, the mechanism nearest this child's `HP:0003202` skeletal muscle atrophy)
   and Elhassan 2019 (bioenergetics) are both explicit nulls, and Shoji 2025 in a
   monogenic progeroid disease found sarcopenia indicators unchanged. That is three
   independent nulls on the endpoint class that matters most here.
3. **The one positive is the weakest design of the set for this purpose.** NICE is in
   peripheral artery disease — a perfusion-limited walking impairment, not a myopathy —
   reports a **one-sided 90% CI** (an unusual and permissive choice), and asks for
   confirmation in its own conclusion. It should not be cited as evidence that NR improves
   muscle in a child with a constitutional protein deficiency.
4. **The consistent positive signal is anti-inflammatory, not myogenic** (Elhassan:
   depressed circulating inflammatory cytokines). If NR has a defensible rationale in this
   case it runs through the SASP/inflammation arm of the fork, **not** through reversing
   muscle atrophy.

**What must change:** NR's rank need not fall on the report's own rubric, but its rationale
must be re-scoped. The muscle-atrophy justification is the part the human evidence
repeatedly fails to support, and it is the part a reader will find most persuasive — so it
is the part most in need of the caveat. This compounds an existing constraint: much of the
growth and muscle deficit here is developmentally fixed, and reversal must not be implied.

## LIMITS — what this file does NOT show

- **These are abstracts, not full texts.** Each is recorded as an abstract-level capture;
  effect sizes, per-protocol handling and secondary endpoints have not been read in full.
  Any claim resting on a specific number here must be upgraded to full text first.
- **None of these trials studied NAD⁺ precursors in BUB1B deficiency, in aneuploidy, or in
  a child.** All enrolled adults; the youngest cohort is 40–70. They constrain the
  *general* muscle claim, not this genotype specifically.
- **A null in an aged or obese adult is not a null in a protein-deficient child.** The
  mechanistic hypothesis here (NAD⁺ → SIRT2 → reduced BubR1 degradation) is genotype-
  specific and is not tested by any of these trials. These are disconfirming for the
  generic muscle-benefit claim, not for the BubR1-abundance hypothesis, which remains
  **untested in humans altogether**.
- No trial here measured BubR1.
