## What it establishes (and the report should cite it for)

The causal chain from aneuploidy to chronic inflammation, stated as a review-level
consensus: "Aneuploidy can induce the pro-inflammatory Senescence Associated Secretory
Phenotype (SASP), either directly or as a result of **micronucleus (MN) induction leading
to leakage of DNA into the cytoplasm and triggering of the cGAS-STING pathway** of innate
immune response." Also: "aneuploidy accompanies the entry into cellular senescence and
can itself induce senescence."

This is the mechanistic sub-chain for the senescence arm of the CIN fork — the step
between "chromosomes missegregate" and "tissue inflammation" — and it is what licenses
considering SASP-directed agents at all.

## The honest limits it states about itself

- "How aneuploidy contributes in vivo to cellular senescence is **less clear**" than in
  vitro. The review's own confidence is graded, and the report should not cite it as
  settled in vivo biology.
- "Due to this complexity, there is at the present time **no biomarker or biomarker
  combination characteristic for all types of senescent cells**." This directly
  constrains the validation plan: any senescence read-out proposed for this child is a
  panel of imperfect proxies, not a definitive assay, and must be described that way.

## How this submission differs from it

Kirsch-Volders & Fenech address **population-level aneuploidy** accumulating with age,
and their proposed intervention is **micronutrient sufficiency** to prevent aneuploidy
arising. That is prevention in people whose chromosome-segregation machinery is intact.

This case is the opposite situation: a child whose segregation machinery is
constitutionally impaired by a defined biallelic genotype, in whom aneuploidy cannot be
prevented at source by nutrition. The intervention question here is therefore
**downstream** (senescence/SASP burden) or **upstream at the protein level** (BubR1
abundance) — neither of which this review addresses. The review supplies the mechanism;
it does not supply the hypothesis.

## Full text (NCBI BioC API, references stripped)

Aneuploidy in exfoliated buccal cells: mechanisms, methods, and future perspectives
Abstract
Aneuploidy and polyploidy have a major impact in congenital and acquired diseases, in particular in
cancer development. This mini-review highlights the use of exfoliated buccal cells as a non-invasive
tool for monitoring aneuploidy. It offers a mechanistic overview and illustrative diagrams
addressing five key areas: (i) the causes of aneuploidy; (ii) cell kinetics and aneuploidy induction
in epithelial buccal cells; (iii) the methods for the detection of aneuploidy; (iv) the scientific
and medical domains applying aneuploidy detection in exfoliated buccal cells; (v) the knowledge gaps
and future research perspectives. Although well validated protocols, automated systems and specific
probes allowing discrimination between chromosome aberration, aneuploidy and polyploidy in
exfoliated buccal cells are available, large-scale cohort human studies remain lacking. These
studies are crucial for evaluating aneuploidy in populations exposed to genotoxic agents or at risk
for buccal dysplasia. Future validation and predictivity studies should compare fluorescence in situ
hybridization and total DNA content methods such as densitometry or flow cytometry, in exposed
individuals and control groups with careful control of confounding factors and adherence to
standardized reporting guidelines. In addition, assessment of aneuploidy frequencies in parallel in
lymphocytes and exfoliated buccal cells in the same populations would allow to compare their
predictive value in both tissues. Moreover, mechanistic studies are also needed to better understand
the sources of variability in aneuploidy and how buccal cell biology compares to other cell types.
In conclusion, due to their ease of collection and non-invasive nature, exfoliated buccal cells
represent a promising tool for aneuploidy testing for risk assessment of environmental or
occupational exposures and disease prediction and monitoring.

## Introduction

Development of valuable methods to assess genetic and/or epigenetic relevant biomarkers is essential
for disease prevention, follow-up, and treatment. Accessibility of the target tissue, specificity of
the genetic/epigenetic endpoint and reproducibility of the assay are determining the choice of the
method. This mini-review focuses on the use of exfoliated buccal cells as a tool to monitor
aneuploidy.
Buccal cells are widely used to assess cellular, nuclear, and genomic characteristics. The major
advantages of studying exfoliated buccal cells are due to the fact that (i) the oral cavity is an
easily accessible area of epithelial tissue of ectodermal origin, (ii) minimally invasive methods
can be applied to obtain buccal epithelial cells, and (iii) they provide interesting in vivo data
that may be relevant to cellular health at the genomic level. Moreover, buccal epithelium receives
an abundant blood supply, through the underlying lamina propria.
Characterization of buccal cells involves changes in nuclear morphology indicative of necrosis or
apoptosis, presence of micronuclei (MN) a biomarker of genotoxicity, binucleation caused by
cytokinesis failure, measurement of DNA content, identification and counting of chromosomes to
assess aneuploidy and polyploidy, whole DNA sequencing and epigenetic profiles.
Aneuploidy corresponds to a chromosome configuration different from the canonical diploid status in
somatic cells (or haploid status in germ cells). Aneuploidy may be due to structural or numerical
chromosome changes. In this review we will consider only numerical whole chromosome aneuploidy.
The impact of aneuploidy on human health is known for more than a century. Theodor Boveri studying
sea urchin embryonic development demonstrated that aneuploidy has a detrimental effect on cell and
organism physiology and further proposed that an abnormal number of chromosomes may promote cancer.
Aneuploidy may occur during the formation of the gametes and is responsible for a significant
percentage of abnormal fetuses and abnormal births (for review). In somatic cells, numerical
aneuploidy may occur spontaneously or be induced by genomic stressors, in particular by chemicals
that may damage one or more components of the mitotic apparatus (for review). In some cases, a
postzygotic mitotic error occurs in one or more blastomeres of the embryo giving birth to a mosaic
individual, carrying aneuploid cells only in some lineages. It is also well known that most cancers
harbor aneuploid cells. However, whether aneuploidy is the cause or consequence of the carcinogenic
process is still a matter of discussion (for review). Presently, aneuploidy is considered as a key
driver of cancer development (for review). However, aneuploidy is not a universal promotor of
tumorigenesis but context-dependent, cancer-type specific oncogenic event. More recently, evidence
for the link between aneuploidy, MN induction, and other diseases is emerging, in particular cardio-
vascular, auto-immune diseases (for review) and also neuro-developmental and brain diseases. In
addition, aneuploidy is found to contribute to cellular senescence and aging (for review).
This mini-review aims to highlight the relevance of aneuploidy assessment in exfoliated buccal
mucosa cells for human health. We therefore will provide mechanistic background and schematic
figures on five major issues: (i) the causes of aneuploidy; (ii) cell kinetics and aneuploidy
induction in epithelial buccal cells; (iii) the methods for the detection of aneuploidy; (iv) the
scientific and medical domains applying aneuploidy detection in exfoliated buccal cells; (v) the
knowledge gaps and future perspectives.

## Causes of aneuploidy

The causes of aneuploidy during meiotic or mitotic divisions are multifaceted, well characterized,
and described in recent reviews. Errors leading to chromosome missegregation may occur in the many
processes controlling the fidelity of nuclear and cell division, either spontaneously, or as a
result of inherited gene mutations or exposure to aneugens. Aneugens, in contrast to clastogens,
have proteins, and not DNA, as target, are able to disturb a large variety of functions and show
thresholded dose–response curves.
The main causes which may lead to aneuploidy during the mitotic process are schematically
illustrated in Fig. 1. At the end of interphase after DNA replication, both sister chromatids are
held together by cohesins. The mitotic cohesin complex forms a ring-shaped structure composed of
four core subunits, RAD21 cohesin complex component (RAD21), structural maintenance of chromosome 3
(SMC3), structural maintenance of chromosome 1A (SMC1A) and stromal antigen 1 and 2 (STAG1 and
STAG2) (for review). During prophase, cohesins which attach both sister chromatids should
progressively be removed by separase to allow chromatid migration to opposite spindle poles during
anaphase. Defects in this process impair sister chromatid segregation (SCS) and thus lead to
aneuploidy. Mutations in cohesin subunit genes -in particular STAG2 nonsense mutations- lead to
aneuploidy and are involved in some cancers. Similarly, mutations in RAD1 were shown to induce
premature chromosome separation and increased aneuploidy (for review). In addition, reduced
expression of cohesin was found to increase chromosome instability (for review). Moreover, cohesion
defects have been linked to the aneuploid phenotype observed in Alzheimer’s disease.
Schematic representation of the causes leading to aneuploidy and the spindle assembly checkpoint
(SAC) during the metaphase/anaphase transition. A/M, actin/myosin; APC/C, anaphase promoting
complex/cyclosome; C, centrosome; MCC, mitotic checkpoint complex; MT, microtubules.
In metaphase, microtubules (MT) originating from each spindle pole attach on the external plate of
the kinetochore (average 10 MT per kinetochore). Kinetochores are disk-like structures that assemble
hundreds of proteins and complexes on centromeric DNA. They comprise an inner region that is tightly
bound to specialized centromeric nucleosomes containing histone variants (for example, the histone 3
variant centromeric protein A (CENP-A/CENH3) and H2A.Z) (for review,) and an outer region that
mediates interaction with MT (for review). However, MT-kinetochore attachment errors may occur such
as merotelic attachment of chromosomes when a single kinetochore binds MT from both spindle poles
(for review,). MT-kinetochore attachment errors may also result from kinetochore complex
deficiencies or aberrant MT dynamics. Treatments with a microtubule-depolymerizing drug such as
nocodazole, or with a microtubule-stabilizing drug such as taxol are well known to induce aneuploidy
(, for review).
During anaphase, migration of the chromatids to the centrosomes is dependent on chemical and
mechanical forces exerted by the MT, and the rigid and tensile actin/myosin cortex. Aberrant MT
and/or actin/myosin dynamics but also aberrant centrosome numbers resulting from abnormal centrosome
amplification or improper time of separation, may lead to chromatid missegregation during anaphase.
Drugs such as monastrol inhibits centrosome separation, producing aberrant MT-kinetochore
attachments (for review,).
Errors may be corrected or, if not corrected, detected by the mitotic spindle assembly checkpoint
(SAC). For instance, merotelic attachments may be resolved into proper biorientation through dynamic
kinetochore-MT turnover (for review,). SAC ensures correct SCS by informing the cell cycle machinery
of potential errors in the interactions of chromosomes with spindle MT prior to anaphase. The SAC
integrates local mechanical and chemical cues, such as unoccupied kinetochores or kinetochores-
lacking tension, and signals errors in a sensitive, responsive, and robust manner. This leads to the
production of the mitotic checkpoint complex (MMC) which inhibits the anaphase-promoting
complex/cyclosome (APC/C) and as a result prevents the destruction of securin by separase and
ensures correct chromatid segregation (for review) (Fig. 1). Once MT-kinetochore attachments are
corrected APC/C is activated and degradation of securin results in sister chromatid separation.
Many key proteins are involved in the activation and silencing of APC/C (for review,) and play
essential roles in controlling of chromatid segregation. They involve among others topoisomerases
and protein kinases (for review). aurora kinase A (AURKA), cyclin-dependent kinase (CDK1), and Polo-
like kinase 1 (PLK1) control centriole duplication and centrosome maturation. Microtubule associated
agents (MTAs), CDK1, AURKA/B, PLK1, and CENP control spindle formation and chromosome attachment.
MTAs, CDK1, AURKAB, PLK1, centromere protein E, budding uninhibited by nenzimidazole 1, monopolar
spindle 1 kinase control chromosome alignment at the metaphase plate. MTAs, PLK1, Pituitary Tumor-
Transforming Gene 1 (securin), extra spindle pole bodies like 1 (separase), kinesins [e.g. KIF2A
(kinesin superfamily protein 2A)] control chromosome separation. MTAs and kinesins control
chromosome segregation at opposite poles, and CDK1, PLK1, aurora kinase B (AURKB), protein regulator
of cytokinesis 1, and Ras homolog family member A (GTPase) control cytokinesis and chromosome
condensation into daughter cells (for review).
In most normal mammalian cells, checkpoints act as a surveillance system that detects chromosome
segregation errors and helps eliminate them by triggering either cell death or cell senescence.
However, inherited mutations, deficiencies in or disturbances by genotoxins of the components of
these checkpoints will allow aneuploid cells to survive or lead to unstable tetraploid intermediates
by mitotic slippage or cytokinesis failure. Many chemicals present in the environment or used for
chemotherapy interact with centrioles, tubulins, centromeres, and represent a risk for induction of
aneuploidy, depending on the target, the dose, and the threshold level for the involved aneugenic
event (; for review). If one of these actors is disturbed, the subsequent cell division may lead to
the formation of two aneuploid daughter cells, one hyperdiploid and one hypodiploid, or to the
induction of a MN (Fig. 2) if the chromosome(s) escapes attachment to the spindle and lags behind in
anaphase/telophase. In the latter situation, the final ploidy level of the daughter cells will
depend on the destiny of the MN: MN may either be reintegrated into the daughter cells at random or
disappear after micro-autophagy, chromosome shattering (for review,) or membrane rupture (for
review) leading to DNA leakage (for review) and pathogenicity (for review).

but might also involve several chromosomes within one or more mitotic cycles.
Aneuploidy may also result as an indirect consequence of polyploidy, in particular tetraploidy.
Tetraploidy, a condition in which a cell has four homologous sets of chromosomes, may be a natural
physiological condition in certain cell types (e.g. hepatocytes, cardiomyocytes, vascular smooth
cells, megakaryocytes) or pathophysiological such as in cancer cells or stress induced
tetraploidisation. Proliferation of tetraploidy cells is suppressed by p53, but cells which have
overcome this barrier show chromosome instability and become aneuploid cells (for review).
Tetraploidy/polyploidy is formed through different mechanisms, such as endoreduplication, cell
fusion, cytokinesis failure or mitotic slippage (for review) (Fig. 2).
As a far as epithelial tissues are concerned, a specific aspect should be taken into consideration.
Knouse et al. discovered that epithelial cells have increased chromosome segregation errors outside
of their native tissues. Using organoid culture systems, they showed that tissue architecture,
specifically integrin-mediated adhesion to the extracellular matrix, is required for accurate
chromosome segregation. In particular, tissue architecture was shown to enhance the correction of
merotelic MT-kinetochores attachment. Whether tissue architecture influences other aspects of the
spindle, such as tension, will require additional experiments. The use of buccal cells exfoliated
from an in vivo situation respecting the epithelial architecture is therefore an important advantage
to assess adequately aneuploidy.

## Cell kinetics and aneuploidy induction in epithelial buccal cells

Since induction of aneuploidy in somatic cells results from abnormal mitotic events, it is crucial
to know where the dividing population of cells is located in the oral mucosa and to understand its
cell kinetics.

## Structure of oral mucosa

The oral mucosa is composed of an outermost layer of stratified squamous epithelium. Below this lies
a basement membrane, followed by a lamina propria and the submucosa as the innermost layer. The
lamina propria contains minor salivary glands, structural fibers, blood vessels, fibroblasts along
with other cell types (for review). The stratified squamous epithelium consists in various layers:
basal, spinous, granular, and corneal layers for the keratinized areas and in non-keratinized areas
the mucosa has basal, spinal, intermediate, and superficial layers. This epithelium is composed of
40–50 cell layers, forming a 500–800 μm thick buccal mucosa. A diagrammatic representation of a
cross section of normal buccal mucosa epithelium can be found in Thomas et al.. For tissue renewal,
DNA repair, and homeostasis, the squamous epithelium covering the oral mucosa relies on epithelial
stem cells.

## Location of stem cells

The lamina propria harbors a unique stem cell population, termed human oral mesenchymal stem cells
(hOMSC). hOMSC have been described to have a primitive neural crest-like phenotypes and can be
induced to differentiate into osteoblastic, chondroblastic, adipocytic, and neuronal lineages (for
review). As far as re-epithelialization is concerned, the epithelial progenitor population is
situated in the basal layer of the oral mucosa. In addition, stem cells are present in the
periodontal and the dental pulp (for review).

## Cell proliferation kinetics

Cell renewal occurs rapidly in oral epithelium, with an average cell cycle time of 2.5–3 days and a
turnover time of 14–24 days for cells to traverse the full epithelial thickness (for review). Cell
proliferation rate varies with anatomical site. Higher cell proliferation (% mean labeling index) is
seen at the floor of the mouth (12.1%), buccal mucosa (10.3%), and vertical tongue sites (10.1%).
Lower activity is found in keratinized palatal mucosa (7.2%) or specialized tongue dorsum (4.3%). In
addition, floor of mouth, ventral, and buccal mucosa all exhibit long S phase durations (7.1–7.4 h)
compared with palate (4.8 h) or tongue dorsum (5.2 h) (for review). Recently, it was demonstrated
that epithelial cell division is controlled by a mechanical G2 checkpoint, which is regulated by
cell-density intercellular forces sensed and transmitted by E-cadherin adhesions. However, how this
might explain the differences in cell cycle duration of the different buccal epithelia is not
understood yet.
Different stem cell proliferation models were proposed. Potten was the first to develop the concept
of the epidermal proliferative unit (EPU), also known as the ‘invariant asymmetry model’. He
hypothesized a central stem cell, presented to be located in the basal cell layer, possessing
lifelong self-renewal properties, thereby maintaining the integrity of epithelial lining tissue.
Undergoing ‘asymmetric division’ of a single stem cell dividing slowly and asymmetrically, always
resulting in a new stem cell and a daughter transit-amplifying replicating cell, which remained
closely associated with one another and are organized in stem cell-transit amplifying cell
territories or progenitor units. This should facilitate both stem cell renewal and clonal production
of subsequently differentiated cells for tissue function (for review).
An alternative cell proliferation hypothesis termed ‘population asymmetry’ model suggests that all
the basal layer cells may function as equipotent stem cells and divide randomly in one or three ways
to form two stem cells, or one stem cell and one differentiating cell, or two differentiating cells.
This implies that tissue homeostasis is maintained at a population rather than an individual cell
level. However, there is little evidence to support this concept (for review).
Jones et al. sought to determine in mice how oral epithelial progenitor cells (OEPCs) are organized,
study the effects of aging and cytotoxic damage on their behavior, and try to resolve which
model—invariant asymmetry or population asymmetry—best explains their division kinetics and clonal
growth patterns. Using lineage tracing and label retention experiments, they showed that rapidly
dividing OEPCs are located broadly within the basal layer of the mucosa throughout the oral cavity.
Moreover, applying quantitative clonal analysis they found that OEPCs undergo population-asymmetric
divisions following neutral drift dynamics and respond to chemotherapy-induced damage by altering
daughter cell fates. Finally, using cell RNA-sequencing they proposed a new model that defines the
organization of cells within the basal layer; mainly, that the oral epithelial basal layer is
comprised of both OEPCs and post-mitotic cells that begun the process of differentiation prior to
leaving the basal layer. Accordingly, one considers that the basal cell layer consists of three
functionally different types of cells: (i) a small population of stem cells, the only basal cells
with essentially unlimited self-renewal capacities, (ii) transit amplifying cells produced by stem
cell division and able to undergo a few rounds of cell division before differentiation, and (iii)
post-mitotic differentiated cells preparing to emigrate from the basal layer (for review).
The cellular and molecular mechanisms of asymmetric stem cell division in tissue homeostasis are not
fully elucidated yet. The asymmetric division of stem cells is achieved by intrinsic and extrinsic
fate determinants such as signaling molecules, epigenetics factors, molecules regulating gene
expression, and polarized organelles (for review). However, at least some stem cells perform
asymmetric and symmetric cell divisions during development.
DNA damage in stem cells was shown to activate p21, inhibit p53, and induce symmetric self-renewing
divisions. The DNA damage response can also initiate mechanisms to limit division of damaged
stem/proliferative cells by inducing apoptosis or a permanent state of cell cycle arrest, known as
senescence. However, stem cells are caught between two opposite needs: on the one hand limit the
propagation of mutations in their progeny cells and on the other hand the responsibility to maintain
tissue regeneration and organization. Therefore, adult stem cells developed a specific sensitivity
or resistance to DNA damage.
During carcinogenesis, asymmetric division of cancer stem cells happens frequently in early stage of
cancer, which not only maintains the pool of cancer cells but also creates tumor heterogeneity. In
the late stage of cancer, the balance between asymmetric and symmetric division in stem cells is
broken. Increase of symmetric division of cancer stem cells allows the tumor to have higher
proliferative capacity. As an example, in oral squamous cell carcinoma, adjacent non-tumor
epithelium, shows loss of asymmetrical proliferative pattern linked to cyclin-D1 overexpression.
Further research is needed to identify the signals that drive OEPCs daughter cell fate behavior and
understand how tissue homeostasis is maintained in oral mucosa.
In summary, as far as induction of aneuploidy is concerned, the data indicate that in buccal mucosa,
cell divisions occur at a relatively high frequency but in a restricted basal part of the
epithelium, both in the stem cells and in the replicating cells. Therefore, one should realize that
only a small proportion of oral mucosa cells are targets for aneuploidy induction in healthy mucosa.

## Methodological issues for the detection of aneuploidy in buccal mucosa


## Exposure routes

Oral mucosa is continuously exposed to environmental, mechanical, and chemical stressors, including
microorganisms and toxins, or ingested antigens, mutagens, and carcinogens. Based on the structure
of buccal mucosa, exposure to physical, chemical, and/or biological stressors is either endogenous
and/or systemic via the blood vessels situated in the lamina propria or exogenous and local via the
mouth. The lamina propria is relatively permeable. Via the mouth, exposure of the stem and
proliferating cells which are localized in the basal layer depends on the permeability of the
numerous epithelial layers separating the oral cavity from the basal layer. The two permeation
pathways for transport across the oral mucosa are paracellular or transcellular. Because the
intercellular spaces and cytoplasm are hydrophilic, lipophilic compounds dissolve poorly in these
regions. The cell membrane, however, is rather lipophilic in nature and hydrophilic solutes will
have difficulties permeating through the cell membrane due to a low partition coefficient. As far as
the nuclear membrane is concerned, it may be lipophilic and allow transit of lipophilic substances
into the nucleus (for review).

## Sample collection location

Data obtained for MN frequencies show that buccal mucosa had the highest percentage of MNs, followed
by the tongue, floor of the mouth, and palate. However, the lowest frequencies were found for the
alveolus and gingiva. It is considered that the level of DNA damage that leads to MN formation and
aneuploidy may depend on the nature of cells in the different locations, with lower or higher level
of keratinization (for review).

## Methods for sampling

Since nuclear division is necessary for aneuploidy induction, sampling should include the basal cell
layer where cell proliferation and regeneration take place. In addition, the daughter cells which
form the differentiating layers provide valuable information on aneuploidy induction. However, since
aneuploidy can be eliminated by apoptosis, the aneuploidy frequencies in differentiating cells may
not reflect the total of aneugenic events which took place in the proliferative cells.
Assessing the frequencies of aneuploid cells in epithelium requires a local biopsy of a small part
of the epithelium including the full basal layer. This is not harmful and sounds easy. However, the
method applied is critical to allow reproducibility and comparison between different sampling times,
individuals, and populations. The most commonly-used cell collection methods, utilizing a toothbrush
or a tongue depressor, produce similar MN frequencies and are discussed in details in several
reviews (for review).

## Choice of aneuploidy assessment method

Assessment of whole chromosome aneuploidy stricto sensu is achieved by counting chromosomes.
Karyotyping of metaphase chromosomes is the traditional approach but requires ex vivo cell division
which is not possible in exfoliated buccal cells. Therefore, accurate aneuploidy assessment in
exfoliated buccal cells applies molecular methods on interphase cells avoiding ex vivo culture.
The use of fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) with chromosome specific (pan-) centromeric,
telomeric (for review) probes or whole chromosome (chromosome painting) (for review) probes allows
counting and/or identification of chromosomes and assessment of aneuploidy. Moreover, with the use
of specific probes and/or combinations, parallel measurement of chromosome breaks, and polyploidy is
feasible (Fig. 3). Detailed technical information for FISH can be found in two review papers. In
addition, high throughput automated technologies are now available for metaphase chromosome counting
based on deep learning and FISH images as well.

An indirect estimation of aneuploidy is feasible by assessing the rate of lagging chromosomes in
anaphase. However, this assay has several caveats including that lagging chromosomes may segregate
correctly and that segregation errors may occur in absence of lagging chromosomes (for review).
Another indirect estimation of aneuploidy is possible through measurement of total nuclear DNA
content via flow cytometry or image analysis and does not require cell division ex vivo. Here also
high throughput automated technologies are now available for DNA content estimation. The major
caveats of these approaches is that total DNA content does not discriminate between G1 or G2 cells,
which might be confusing to assess polyploidy, and requires statistical methods to define the ploidy
ratio which is indicative for aneuploidy.
The advantages and disadvantages of those methods are nicely reviewed by Bakker et al. and
summarized in Table 1 for comparison. Bakker et al. clearly conclude that the most affordable
methods require dividing cells, in which case, aneuploidy rates are only representative of the
dividing subpopulation, and not of the whole cell population. While methods quantifying aneuploidy
rates in interphase cells may circumvent this bias, flow cytometry or image analysis cannot detect
aneuploidies at the single cell level and FISH is limited to analysis of a few chromosomes per cell
(except with individual chromosome painting).

For these different reasons, whole genome sequencing (WGS) methods are now increasingly used also
for aneuploidy/polyploidy assessment. They provide comprehensive genome information by high-

allowing determination of genomic content and karyotypes at high resolution in an unbiased and high
throughput fashion. At the present time, aneuploidy/ploidy estimation methods for bulk and single-
cell WGS are applied with different types of next-generation sequencing tools and numerous
computational tools (for review,). However, their validation is not fully achieved yet, as described
in two recent reviews. Salam et al. provided a detailed overview of the current trends and
advancements in next generation sequencing technology in a recent review. Song et al. made a
benchmarking comparison of ploidy estimation based on 11 methods for bulk WGS and 8 methods for
single-cell WGS data; they provide valuable guidance for future research on aneuploid/ploidy
analysis and ongoing improvements in computational tools for single-cell sequencing data (for
review,).

## Scientific and medical domains applying aneuploidy assessment in exfoliated buccal cells

As nicely summarized in a recent reviews by Fenech et al. and Bolognesi et al., assessment of
genomic changes in exfoliated buccal cells includes morphological changes such as micronuclei (MN),
and specific chromosomal and ploidy changes (Fig. 2). The validation of the protocols for sampling
of exfoliated buccal cells and MN assessment (for review), the easy accessibility of the target
tissue and the low invasiveness of the exfoliated method is leading to the increasing use of buccal
cells in constantly broader scientific and medical domains. This is nicely shown in a time-line of
the HUMN project achievements with the buccal MN assay.
As far as aneuploidy in exfoliated buccal cells is concerned, the early data focused on constitutive
aneuploidies assessed with FISH. Identification of aneuploidy at individual level with FISH requires
specific probes, hybridization protocols and, in case of epithelial tissue such as buccal cells, an
adequate penetration protocol, issues which are well validated.
At population level, the evaluation of occupational and environmental exposure to genotoxicants can
be assessed in exfoliated buccal cells with FISH or with total nuclear DNA quantification by
cytometry or image analysis. However, the data is scarce. Exposure to benzene, a compound known to
be potentially both clastogenic and aneugenic, is reported only by Surrallés et al.. In this study,
18 workers (exposed to benzene) from an Estonian petrochemical plant and 15 age-matched controls
were examined. The researchers compared the frequency of MN (clastogenic and aneugenic effects), the
presence of MN containing whole chromosomes versus acentric fragments using a pan-centromeric DNA
probe in lymphocytes and exfoliated buccal cells, and chromosome 9-specific FISH to detect numerical
abnormalities of chromosome 9 in exfoliated buccal cells. At the estimated benzene exposure levels
(8 h time-weighted average) no increase of total MN, MN harboring whole chromosomes or acentric
fragments or chromosome 9 numerical abnormalities were detected. Other variables, such as age,
smoking or alcohol consumption, did not influence any of the multiple cytogenetic biomarkers
assessed.

studies. Table 2 lists the publications assessing aneuploidy in exfoliated buccal cells from smokers

are challenging to interpret due to the limited number of available cases, incomplete information on
potential confounding factors and/or heterogeneity of the technologies used to estimate aneuploidy.
In fact, well designed studies involving larger populations and accounting for relevant confounders
are lacking. Additionally, further research is needed to determine the most appropriate FISH

More recently, the use of buccal cells gained importance at individual level to diagnose and manage
newborns with abnormal genomic constitution such as Turner syndrome and mosaicism, patients with
Fanconi anemia and to follow up medical therapies. Research at population level is progressing
toward the assessment of aging and associated diseases such as Alzheimer’s disease and, in
particular, to the development of prognostic biomarkers for malignant progression of oral epithelial
dysplasia (OED). Several studies were performed on biopsies from patients at various stages of
malignant progression, using different methods for aneuploidy assessment. As recent examples,
Hussein et al. reported that DNA flow cytometric analysis of oral carcinomas excisions is a
significant prediction of advanced metastatic stages in oral malignant disorders. Li et al.
performed a prospective diagnostic study and assessed DNA aneuploidy with image cytometry to detect
dysplasia and carcinoma in oral potentially malignant disorders. Their findings suggest that DNA-
image cytometry may serve as a useful noninvasive tool for identifying both oral cancer and its
precursor lesions. These conclusions were confirmed in a recent paper by Li et al.. Odell compared
aneuploidy and loss of heterozygosity as risk markers for malignant transformation in oral mucosa
and found that comparisons are challenging because of differences in how results are reported and
application to populations of different risks. Overall, these studies suggest that aneuploidy
detected in patient biopsies may be is predictive for oral malignancy grading but a systematic
comparative analysis is still missing. Recently, Turton et al. conducted a systematic review aimed
to evaluate advances in prognostic biomarkers that may predict malignant progression of OED. They
investigated 32 biomarkers, 20 of which demonstrated significant potential for predicting OED
progression. A meta-analysis further confirmed the significant prognostic value of four specific 4
biomarkers: podoplanin, epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) expression, p16 methylation, and DNA
aneuploidy. However, they concluded that further research is required, to validate the most
promising biomarkers in large cohort studies, with adherence to standardized reporting guidelines.
Although these studies are performed on buccal biopsies and not on exfoliated buccal cells, they
provide support for the potential predictivity of aneuploidy scoring in exfoliated buccal cells for
early detection of malignant transformation.
Furthermore, it was also recently shown that using molecular probes it is possible to test whether
numerical chromosome instability increases the risk of malignant transformation in oral lichen
planus. The results suggested that aneuploidy alone (of chromosomes 2 and 8) may not be a strong
predictor of cancer risk in lichen planus. However, this may be because the chromosomes need to be
shattered in a micronucleus and then the fragments tethered into a nucleus and rearranged within the
nucleus before the chromosomes become oncogenic. It may also depend on which chromosomes are
involved and which of them were trapped within a micronucleus. The results may have been different
if probes for other chromosomes were used, and identified which chromosomes tended to be entrapped
and shattered within a MN because this not only increases structural rearrangements in the
chromosomes but also epigenetic alterations.

## Knowledge gaps and future perspectives

Cytological, chromosomal, and molecular methods for assessing aneuploidy in exfoliated buccal cells
are available, technically validated and applied with various purposes. When used at the individual
level to identify or monitor genomic alterations—provided that appropriate probes are employed—these
methods generally do not present significant challenges. Their use at population level and, in
particular, their predictivity for a given disease, requires additional research. This includes
validating techniques such as FISH and total DNA quantification methods (via densitometry or flow
cytometry) in large cohort studies, with well-matched controls for potential confounding factors and
with adherence to standardized reporting guidelines. In the near future, single-cell WGS is expected
to provide a useful approach to detect aneuploidy. Moreover, it will allow to compare data both on
gene mutations and on aneuploidy/polyploidy in the same cell.
Well-designed studies on cohorts exposed to genotoxic substances versus controls should provide
valuable information about the predictivity for risk estimation. Moreover, in a first step, a
priority should be given to populations exposed to known aneugens, such as spindle or SAC inhibitors
(for review) before validation on complex mixtures. As far as predictivity for onset and malignant
progression of OED is concerned, similar recommendations are applicable.
If the data confirm that assessing aneuploidy in exfoliated buccal cells is effective for evaluating
systemic or local exposures, this approach could serve as an important biomarker. In a subsequent
phase, it will be necessary to analyze whether aneuploidy assessment in exfoliated buccal cells
offers predictive value comparable to that of human lymphocytes for risk evaluation and diseases
prediction. In this perspective, the aforementioned study on benzene exposure levels was well
designed. The study compared total MN, MN containing whole chromosomes or acentric fragments in
lymphocytes and buccal cells, as well as numerical abnormalities of chromosome 9 in buccal cells. No
increase in any assessed biomarkers was observed at the measured level benzene exposure. Other
variables, such as age, smoking or alcohol consumption, did not influence any of the multiple
cytogenetic biomarkers evaluated. Some recent studies compared the predictivity of MN in buccal
cells versus lymphocytes and concluded that it deserves additional research.
In addition to the cohort studies, some mechanistic studies should be recommended on the sources of
variability causing aneuploidy, and how buccal cell biology compares to other cell types.
Understanding of aneuploidy kinetics and induction could be further strengthened by empirical
studies with relevant parameters and comparing findings between populations.

## Conclusion

Exfoliated buccal cells provide important genetic information on constitutive or induced genomic
constitution, to assess occupational or environmental exposure, and to predict and follow up
diseases. Considering the major impact of aneuploidy and polyploidy in congenital and acquired
diseases, in particular in cancer development, the assessment of those endpoints in exfoliated
buccal cells is a promising approach. Although well validated protocols, automated systems and
specific probes allowing discrimination between chromosome aberration, aneuploidy and polyploidy are
available, the absence of valuable cohort studies on populations exposed to relevant genotoxic
substances or on OED patients leads to major shortcomings. Assessing predictivity for genotoxic
exposure and disease requires additional research, including the validation of FISH, total DNA
methods by densitometry or flow cytometry and/or single-cell WGS in large cohort studies, with well-
matched controls for confounding factors in adherence to standardized reporting guidelines. Whether
aneuploidy in exfoliated buccal cells is more informative than in other cell types, in particular
lymphocytes, requires further research.
