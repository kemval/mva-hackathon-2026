## What it shows

"Senescent fat progenitor cells accumulate in adipose tissue with aging and acquire a
senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) ... The JAK pathway is activated in
adipose tissue with aging, and **the SASP can be suppressed by inhibiting the JAK
pathway** in senescent cells. **JAK1/2 inhibitors reduced inflammation and alleviated
frailty in aged mice.**"

## Why a senomorphic tier is worth having at all

It targets a different point in the chain from senolytics: senolytics remove the cells,
senomorphics silence their output. In a child in whom senescent cells may be arising
continuously — because the underlying missegregation never stops — a one-off "hit and
run" clearance has an obvious limitation that continuous SASP suppression does not.

## Why it nevertheless ranks below the abundance tier

- **Aged wild-type mice, not a BubR1 model.** Nothing here concerns chromosome
  segregation, BubR1, MVA, or children.
- **Immunosuppression is the mechanism, not a side effect.** JAK1/2 inhibition dampens
  interferon and cytokine signalling systemically. In a child with a
  cancer-predisposition syndrome — and in light of Bakhoum 2018's finding that
  cGAS-STING sensing of micronuclei is entangled with tumour behaviour — suppressing
  innate-immune surveillance is a substantive risk, not a minor caveat.
- It treats a **consequence** while leaving the cause untouched.

Approved JAK inhibitors with paediatric labelling exist, which is what keeps this tier on
the list at all; the specific agents, their paediatric indications and their safety
profiles are resolved in the candidate dossiers rather than asserted here.

## Abstract (eutils)

## Abstract

A hallmark of aging is chronic sterile inflammation, which is closely associated with frailty and
age-related diseases. We found that senescent fat progenitor cells accumulate in adipose tissue with
aging and acquire a senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP), with increased production of
proinflammatory cytokines compared with nonsenescent cells. These cells provoked inflammation in
adipose tissue and induced macrophage migration. The JAK pathway is activated in adipose tissue with
aging, and the SASP can be suppressed by inhibiting the JAK pathway in senescent cells. JAK1/2
inhibitors reduced inflammation and alleviated frailty in aged mice. One possible mechanism
contributing to reduced frailty is SASP inhibition. Our study points to the JAK pathway as a
potential target for countering age-related dysfunction.

## What the full text adds beyond the abstract

**SASP suppression is partial and segmental — not abolition.** At 0.6 µM, JAK inhibitor 1
"suppressed the mRNA levels of key SASP components, including IL-6, IL-8, and MCP-1, **by
up to 60%**," and "Increasing the concentration of JAK inhibitor 1 to 1 µM **did not
further suppress the SASP**." In the recipient-cell assay: "Interestingly, **IL-1α, IL-1β,
and IL-8 expression did not change** with JAK inhibitor treatment, suggesting that the
**SASP is segmentally suppressed** by JAK inhibitors." Any claim that JAK inhibition
"blocks the SASP" overstates this paper; it partially and selectively damps it, with a
ceiling.

**The in vivo experiment is 24-month-old wild-type mice.** "We treated 24-mo-old C57BL/6
male mice with ruxolitinib (INCB18424), a selective JAK1/2 inhibitor approved by the Food
and Drug Administration, or vehicle (DMSO) **for 10 wk**." Of seven cytokines that rose
with age, "JAK inhibitor treatment significantly decreased **five** of these targets."

**Ruxolitinib is a senomorphic here, not a senolytic.** The paper's design silences
secretion; it does not claim to remove senescent cells. Xu 2015 and the D+Q senolytic
papers are therefore **not interchangeable evidence**, and a report that ranks them in one
list must keep the mechanisms distinct.

**The paper's own safety discussion, verbatim and unflattering.** "Roxolitinib can have
side effects, including **anemia and thrombocytopenia**, in humans who have myelofibrosis.
We did not observe an alteration of red blood cell numbers or hemoglobin levels in old
mice treated with roxolitinib, although **platelet counts tended to be reduced** (not
significantly; n = 9)." The authors' mitigating argument is selectivity: ruxolitinib "is a
selective JAK1/2 inhibitor that has much less effect on JAK3 and TYK2, the two JAK family
members associated with immune cell generation and function."

## LIMITS — what this paper does NOT show

- **No BubR1, no aneuploidy, no MVA.** Senescence here is induced by irradiation or serial
  passage in human preadipocytes and HUVECs, and by natural ageing in rodents. Nothing
  connects it to chromosome missegregation as the senescence trigger.
- **No young or paediatric organism anywhere.** Every in vivo experiment is in aged (24-mo
  mouse, 30-mo rat) animals, and the human primary cells came from donors aged 71 ± 2 and
  31 ± 5. The entire rationale is *reversing an age-accumulated burden* — a different
  situation from a growing child in whom senescent cells may be generated continuously
  from birth.
- **Adipose tissue is the studied compartment**, not skeletal muscle. Frailty and physical
  function were measured; muscle histology and BubR1 were not.
- **Ruxolitinib is an approved medication, but not for this.** It is approved for
  myelofibrosis and related indications; using it here would be off-label repurposing, and
  the trial evidence in this paper is entirely preclinical.
- **The paper cannot address tumour risk in either direction.** Given this proband's
  rhabdomyosarcoma history, sustained suppression of JAK/STAT inflammatory signalling in a
  child with a cancer-predisposition syndrome is an unquantified risk that this paper
  neither measures nor rules out. Per the fork constraint, no senescence- or SASP-directed
  agent should be presented as reducing tumour risk — and this one raises a question about
  the opposite direction that the literature does not answer (see
  `literature/_open_questions.md`, **Q5**).

## Evidence tier

**Mouse (non-BubR1 background) + human primary cell culture.** No human trial of this
intervention for this purpose; no paediatric data.
