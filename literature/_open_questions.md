# Open Questions the Literature Cannot Answer

Ranked by **decision value** — how much the answer would change what is recommended for
this child — not by scientific interest. Each carries the cheapest experiment that would
settle it.

No question here proposes recontact with the family, the clinical team, or
patient-organisation contacts. Q1–Q3 are bench assays on material that either already
exists or is obtainable through routine clinical care.

---

## Q1 — What is this proband's actual residual BubR1 protein level? **[decision value: maximal]**

**Why the literature cannot answer it.** Suijkerbuijk 2010 establishes a functional
threshold between **~6% and ~13%** of normal, but in *other* patients with *other*
variants. No published work measures BubR1 in this genotype
(p.Leu737Ter / p.Asn1002Lys), and the pseudokinase-domain missense variant is a gnomAD
singleton with no functional characterisation anywhere.

**What it forks.** Everything. Above threshold, the abundance-raising hypothesis has little
headroom and the case for any intervention weakens. Below it, the hypothesis is live and
the target is quantified. It also determines whether a small further decrement — the C2
quercetin worry — is tolerable or not.

**Cheapest experiment.** **Western blot for BubR1 on patient-derived fibroblasts or
lymphoblasts**, against parental and unrelated controls. Standard equipment, days of
bench time, no new sample if a cultured line already exists.

**Bonus, and it is substantial.** A quantitative deficit demonstrated for the missense
allele generates **PS3-grade functional evidence** — which is precisely what Track 1's
unresolved VUS lacks. One assay serves both tracks. This is already the report's
first recommendation and this review reaffirms it as correctly placed.

## Q2 — Does any NAD⁺ precursor raise BubR1 in a **human**? **[decision value: high]**

**Why the literature cannot answer it.** North 2014 measured BubR1 after NMN in
**wild-type mouse testes** at 500 mg/kg/day **intraperitoneally**. No human study has ever
measured BubR1 after NR or NMN — not Shoji 2025, not Veenhuis 2021, not any of the four
trials in `30_`. The lead candidate's central mechanistic premise is unmeasured in humans.

**What it forks.** Whether NR has a genotype-specific rationale at all, or only the generic
anti-inflammatory effect of C5 — which is a much weaker basis for recommending it in a
child.

**Cheapest experiment.** **BubR1 western on patient fibroblasts cultured ± NR at
physiologically achievable concentrations** (single-digit µM, matched to reported human
plasma levels — *not* the mouse i.p. exposure). Same assay and same cells as Q1; the
marginal cost is a few extra culture conditions.

**Note.** An ex vivo rise would be encouraging but would not establish clinical benefit —
C3 shows target engagement and functional benefit repeatedly dissociate in humans.

## Q3 — Does quercetin lower BubR1 at achievable exposures? **[decision value: high, and it is a safety question]**

**Why the literature cannot answer it.** Aalinkeel 2008 shows quercetin makes HSP90 the
most-suppressed protein in the proteome at **50 µM** in a cancer cell line. No study
measures BubR1 under quercetin at any concentration, and no study bridges from 50 µM in
vitro to oral dosing in a child. The potency argument (C2) says the risk is probably
negligible — but "probably" is doing load-bearing work in a patient sitting near a 6–13%
threshold.

**What it forks.** Whether dasatinib + quercetin can stand as the highest-scoring approved
medication, or must carry an explicit caution. Also whether gate G1's exposure qualifier is
correctly calibrated.

**Cheapest experiment.** **BubR1 and HSP90-client (Raf-1, cdk4) westerns on patient
fibroblasts across a quercetin dose range spanning achievable plasma levels through
50 µM.** A dose-response that is flat at achievable concentrations closes the question
cleanly; one that is not would be a genuinely important negative result. Again the same
cells and assay as Q1 — **Q1, Q2 and Q3 are one experiment with three arms.**

## Q4 — Are HSP90-inhibitor senolytics actually harmful here, or only predicted to be? **[decision value: high, and currently prohibitive]**

**Why the literature cannot answer it.** The contraindication (D5) is an inference across
three papers — Suijkerbuijk (mutant BubR1 is an HSP90 client), Tang (aneuploid cells depend
on protein quality control), Fuhrmann-Stroissnigg (HSP90 inhibitors are senolytics). No
study has ever exposed BubR1-deficient or MVA cells to an HSP90 inhibitor and measured
BubR1 or viability. The prediction is well-founded and untested.

**What it forks.** Nothing, in practice — and this is the rare case where an untested
prediction should still be acted on. The asymmetry is decisive: if the prediction is right,
the harm is depleting the protein the child's checkpoint function depends on; if wrong, the
cost of avoidance is forgoing one investigational drug class among several. **Avoidance is
correct under uncertainty.** The question is worth answering for the field, not for this
child's next decision.

**Cheapest experiment.** Patient fibroblasts ± 17-DMAG or geldanamycin at senolytic
concentrations, BubR1 western plus viability. A fourth arm on the same plate as Q1–Q3.

## Q5 — Does SASP or JAK/STAT suppression impair tumour immunosurveillance in a child already predisposed to cancer? **[decision value: moderate — but it gates a whole tier]**

**Why the literature cannot answer it.** Xu 2015 reports ruxolitinib's haematological
effects and argues JAK1/2 selectivity spares immune-cell generation, but it studies **aged
wild-type mice**, measures inflammation and frailty, and never addresses tumour risk.
Mackenzie 2017 and Bakhoum 2018 establish that micronuclei drive cGAS-STING signalling —
the same axis a senomorphic would damp — but in cancer-cell contexts, not in constitutional
aneuploidy. Nothing joins these.

**What it forks.** Whether the senomorphic tier is admissible **at all** in a child with a
rhabdomyosarcoma history. Note carefully: this is *not* the claim that senescence causes
cancer here — B1/B4 forbid that, and the fork constraint holds. It is the distinct and
legitimate question of whether suppressing inflammatory signalling has an **iatrogenic**
effect on immune surveillance.

**Cheapest experiment.** None is cheap, and that is the honest answer. The tractable
substitute is a **structured safety review of malignancy incidence in the existing
paediatric JAK-inhibitor population** (juvenile idiopathic arthritis, atopic dermatitis),
where exposure data already exist — a registry analysis, not a new experiment. It would not
be definitive for a cancer-predisposition syndrome, but it is the only proportionate route.

## Q6 — Does the aneuploidy→senescence link hold **in vivo**, in this disease? **[decision value: moderate — it underwrites the whole senescence arm]**

**Why the literature cannot answer it.** Kirsch-Volders & Fenech state it plainly against
their own thesis: "**How aneuploidy contributes in vivo to cellular senescence is less
clear.**" And there is no validating read-out even in principle — "there is at the present
time **no biomarker or biomarker combination characteristic for all types of senescent
cells**." The senescence arm rests on in vitro evidence plus one mouse model (Baker 2011)
whose benefit is explicitly tissue-restricted (B5).

**What it forks.** Whether senescence burden is a real therapeutic target in this child or
an extrapolation from ageing biology. If it is not engaged, the entire senolytic and
senomorphic tier is moot regardless of which agent is chosen.

**Cheapest experiment.** A **panel** of senescence proxies on patient fibroblasts —
SA-β-gal, p16INK4a, p21Cip1, γH2AX, plus micronucleus scoring — against age-matched
controls. It must be reported as a panel of imperfect proxies, never as a definitive assay,
because the review above says no definitive assay exists.

## Q7 — What does the 2023 Kirsch-Volders review actually say beyond its abstract? **[decision value: low — but it is an open provenance gap]**

**Why it is unresolved.** Elsevier; no open-access deposit located; NCBI's own PMC link
resolves to a different paper (C7). Only the abstract is held. Recorded here so the gap is
visible rather than quietly reasoned around.

**Cheapest route.** Institutional or library access. Until then, no claim in this
repository may quote past the verified abstract — a constraint now enforced in file `18_`.

## What would change the recommendation, ranked

1. **Q1** — a BubR1 level. Everything downstream is conditional on it.
2. **Q3** — a flat quercetin dose-response would clear the report's top approved medication; a
   non-flat one would demote it.
3. **Q2** — a null would reduce NR to its generic anti-inflammatory rationale.
4. **Q5** — a positive safety signal would close the senomorphic tier.
5. **Q6** — a negative would make the entire senescence arm moot.
6. **Q4** — will not change this child's management; avoidance is already correct.
7. **Q7** — provenance completeness only.

**Q1, Q2, Q3 and Q4 are four arms of a single fibroblast experiment.** That is the
single most cost-effective piece of new evidence available in this case, and it is
obtainable with a western blot.

## Q8 — When was this child's first renal ultrasound, and what did it show? **[decision value: high for Track 1, zero for Track 2]**

**Why it matters.** The entire Track 1 nephrocalcinosis argument turns on whether *"present
since birth"* is a dated imaging observation or a summary-table compression. In preterm
infants the literature scans first at 1 month, diagnoses at 35 weeks corrected GA, and reports
a mean age at first diagnosis of 385 days (`literature/31_...`). A single date would settle
which of the two readings is right.

**Why the literature cannot answer it.** It is a fact about one child, not about a
population. No published source can supply it.

**Cheapest experiment.** One line in the radiology record: the date and finding of the first
renal ultrasound. **This analysis cannot obtain it — recontact with the family or clinical
team is prohibited** — so it is recorded here as a genuinely open question, not a task.

**What to do without it.** State both readings and their consequences, and do not let either
carry a conclusion. Note that the *direction-of-inference* error identified in matrix C13 is
independent of this question and stands regardless of how it resolves.

## Q9 — Does cancer surveillance in MVA change after a first malignancy? **[decision value: high — and it is the question a family will actually ask]**

**Why it matters.** The protocol this project recommends is a *Wilms* protocol: renal
ultrasound every 3 months, birth→7 years. This child has already had a rhabdomyosarcoma. A
first tumour in a predisposition syndrome plausibly changes both the risk and the target — and
prior cancer-directed therapy is itself a risk factor for subsequent neoplasms — but the
guideline addresses none of this.

**Why the literature cannot answer it.** The MVA recommendation is silent (`literature/32_...`,
claim J2). With under 50 described patients and a guideline that says its own rarity "hampers
establishment of an accurate risk assessment," there is no cohort from which a post-first-tumour
protocol could be derived.

**Cheapest experiment — and it is not an experiment.** Two tractable steps, neither requiring
patient contact: (i) retrieve **COG LTFU v6.0** from survivorshipguidelines.org and check
whether its new genetic-predisposition sections name MVA or BUB1B (claim J9); (ii) obtain
**Hol et al. 2021** through institutional access and check whether the primary SIOP-E text
addresses post-first-tumour surveillance where Nakano's summary does not (claim J7). Beyond
those, this needs a registry — which is precisely what the SIOP-E authors call for.

**What to say meanwhile.** That the protocol on offer was written for a different situation
than the one this family is in. That is honest, useful, and is not a recommendation to deviate
from it.
